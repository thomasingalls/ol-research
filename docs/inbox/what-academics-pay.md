## 1. Why this sweep

The user: "none of this feels particularly original, except perhaps
applying these methods to academic literature search at accessible
prices. or at any price, seems like this is maybe an underserved market."
The conversation's answer was that underserved markets are usually
underserved for a reason, that academics pay little, and that the
question is who pays, how much, and through whom.

**What researchers do with them** (Wiley's ExplanAItions 2025, [P],

have access to a paid one; 40 % say their organisation provides AI tools

tools surveyed. Elsevier's 2025 survey ([P]): 58 % use AI, 27 % feel
adequately trained. **Researchers paying out of pocket or from grants:
not found.**

## 3. Systematic reviews and screening

([P] each)

- **Covidence:** $339 a year for one review, $907 for three; departmental
  and organisation-wide licences by quote; reduced rates for low- and
  middle-income countries; owned by a non-profit, the Future Evidence
  Foundation; Cochrane's standard platform since 2015, and 800,000+
  researchers at 500+ institutions by its own count.
- **Rayyan:** free for 3 active reviews; $4.99–8.33 a month; a
  business or academic plan at $41.67 a licence a month, 5 minimum.
- **DistillerSR:** students $19.95, faculty $94 a month; departments,
  institutions and companies by quote.
- **EPPI-Reviewer:** £10 a user a month plus £35 a shared review a month;
  free for Cochrane and Campbell authors.
- **Nested Knowledge:** academic $19.95 and $69.95 a user a month;
  corporate $295 and $695.
- **ASReview:** free and open source, run from Utrecht on a €1.5M grant.
- **Laser AI:** unverified (the page showed one figure for every tier).

**What a review costs, as the frame for what screening saves:** about
$141,195 per review (Michelson and Reuter, 2019, [A]); a mean of 67.3
weeks and 5 authors over 195 registered reviews (Borah et al., BMJ Open
2017, [A]).

## 4. Institutions, libraries and consortia

**France** (Couperin's survey of electronic resources for 2024,
[P]): 311 institutions spent €130.4M before tax, up 1 % on 2023:
periodicals €78.7M (60 %), databases €37.6M (29 %), e-books €9.7M (8 %).
79 % went through Couperin's negotiations; CNRS was the largest single
spender at €10.0M. National group purchases:

| | 2020 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Web of Science + InCites | €3.7M | €4.6M | €4.7M | €4.6M |
| Scopus + SciVal | €2.1M | €2.2M | €2.2M | €1.7M |

The 2024 fall in Scopus coincides with CNRS leaving it (the
researcher's reading). Offers for both for 2027–2029 were with Couperin's
members as of 24–25 September 2026 ([P]).

**How a vendor gets in** (Couperin, [P]): propose in February or March
of the year before; a negotiation is "pointless" without about a dozen
interested institutions; answer Couperin's GDPR and AI questionnaires
(the AI one updated 2026-09-18) and supply KBART data, COUNTER 5 usage
statistics and the standard licence; members get trials of one to three
months. Couperin is a non-profit funded by its members' dues and a
ministry subsidy.

**Europe:** in 2016–17, the top three database deals per country across
28 consortia came to €33.3M a year, against €383.6M for periodicals;
68 % were bought by a consortium and recharged to institutions (EUA Big
Deals Survey 2018, [P]). EU procurement thresholds for 2026–27: €140,000
for supplies and services bought by central government, €216,000 by
other public bodies (Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2152, [P]); France's
own lower threshold was not verified.

**Libraries and AI:** 3 % of 1,876 libraries report active AI
implementation and 30 % have no plans (Clarivate's Pulse of the Library
2026, a vendor's survey, [P]).

**A nuance to the notes:** the industry note's "academia absent as a
buyer" holds for licensing text to AI, not for discovery, where French
institutions alone spent €37.6M on databases in 2024.

## 5. The move to open infrastructure

- **Sorbonne University** declined to renew Web of Science and Clarivate's
  bibliometric tools for 2024 and set out to partner with OpenAlex
  (announced 2023-12-08, [P]).
- **CNRS** ended its Scopus subscription on 2023-12-31, kept Web of
  Science for its higher use, and points researchers to OpenAlex,
  Crossref, Dimensions and HAL (announced 2024-01-11, [P]).
- **France's open-science strategy for 2026–2033**, launched 2026-09-16
  ([PDF](https://doi.org/10.52949/93), [P]), asks institutions to
  promote tools like OpenAlex and to contribute to its economic model
  (measure OS4.A1.M1), and counts the French institutions that do; it
  encourages the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information.
- **The Barcelona Declaration** has 146 signatories and 57 supporters;
  France's 18 signatories include Université Paris-Saclay, Sorbonne,
  the ANR and Couperin (since 2026-03-09); CNRS is not among them ([P]).

The researcher's reading: the policy is to move money to open
infrastructure, while national renewals of the proprietary databases run
to 2029; a newcomer built on open data will be judged against "OpenAlex
plus a membership fee".

## 6. Companies pay more for the same tools

Nested Knowledge charges about 15 times more for a corporate seat than
an academic one ($295 against $19.95); DistillerSR publishes only its
academic prices; scienceOS's list prices exclude commercial use;
Consensus sells to research organisations separately; Elicit, Undermind
and Laser AI show no academic tier ([P]). The researcher's reading:
price discrimination, with academia as the funnel and companies paying
the margin; that academia is a loss leader is shown only indirectly.

## 7. What this suggests

Held loosely, as the label says.

- **Money flows through three doors, at three sizes.** Individuals pay
  $10–50 a month, and most use free tools anyway (§2). Review teams pay
  per review or per seat, hundreds of dollars a year (§3). Institutions
  pay millions, through consortia, on a yearly calendar, with GDPR and AI
  questionnaires at the door (§4). Each door wants a different product
  and a different sale.
- **The consortium door is open and has a timetable.** Couperin publishes
  how to enter: propose in February–March, find a dozen interested
  institutions, answer the questionnaires, offer trials. For a
  Paris-based product that is a concrete path, and the questionnaires are
  worth reading before the product is built.
- **Open infrastructure is the policy, and it sets the bar.** France is
  steering money towards OpenAlex; Paris-Saclay has signed the Barcelona
  Declaration. This project is built on open data (Crossref, PubMed,
  OpenAlex), which fits the policy; but it will be priced against "open
  data plus a membership fee", so what it charges for has to be what open
  data alone does not give: measured recall, taught methods, freshness.
- **Europe values data protection.** European tools sell on EU servers,
  data processing agreements and GDPR; on-box models
  ([teach it your method](teach-it-your-method.md) §8) are an asset
  here, not only a cost choice.
- **The low price only works with low costs.** At $10–50 a month, or
  inside a consortium's budget line, the product has to be cheap to
  serve: the cost bet and the market bet are one bet (status).

**A detail for the extension notes:** Paris-Saclay's library says its
Lean Library browser extension will "no longer be operational from 1
January 2027"; Lean Library itself shows no shutdown and Couperin lists a
2027 negotiation, so this is probably a local non-renewal (the
researcher's reading). A library-access extension leaving the campus
next door is worth knowing about for the project's own browser
extension.
